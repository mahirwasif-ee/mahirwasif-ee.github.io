Reflections

Reflection 1

EE 591: Analog Integrated Circuits

Taught by Professor Sahoo

Analog circuits are still essential because they connect real-world signals to digital systems. This course showed me how design choices affect performance, and the math behind transistor circuits was the hardest part. Differential amplifiers, current mirrors, and op-amps felt difficult at first, but with practice I began to see how the concepts connect to real circuit behavior.

I also had to learn Linux and set up the Cadence environment. Analog design is about balance: power, noise, bandwidth, and stability all affect each other. Reading the graphs was really hard, but simulating the op-amp taught me how gain and phase change with frequency and how to judge bandwidth and stability from a Bode plot.

The biggest challenge was learning what goes into getting a circuit ready for fabrication: finding errors, tracing their cause, fixing the design, and checking everything again. Professor Sahoo sometimes added errors to our circuits for us to find. I was scared of that at first, but it became a game. This course made me realize how much I enjoy analog design and want to keep learning it.

Reflection 2

EE 550: Hardware Architecture for Machine Learning

Taught by Professor Sahoo

This was the hardest and most interesting project of my academic life: an 8-bit fully differential SAR ADC in TSMC 180 nm. Every stage stacked on the last, and every block had its own way of breaking. A SAR architecture is low power and small, and going fully differential cancels a lot of common-mode noise. We used a 1.2 V high reference and 0.6 V low reference centered at 0.9 V, aimed at bio-signals like ECG and PPG.

The capacitive DAC taught me the most. It uses binary-weighted MOM capacitors with a unit cap near 6.35 fF plus one dummy, summing to 256C. Each capacitor has a transmission gate for sampling, a PMOS to the high reference, and an NMOS to the low reference, which gives a clean swing without a threshold drop. Clock generation mattered just as much: the sampling and decision phases cannot overlap, and we derived every phase from one master clock with clean, non-overlapping waveforms.

The full FFT testbench gave an ENOB around 7.96 bits and SNR near 49 dB after layout, against about 8 bits pre-layout. Layout was the grind: we drew every block in Cadence and wrapped the chip in a pad ring. The SAR logic block alone took a week to make DRC and LVS clean, and seeing zero errors was a huge relief. This project is the reason I know what I want to focus my career on.

Reflection 3

EE 550: Digital Integrated Circuits

Taught by Professor Sahoo

Most of the semester was slow, careful work: building logic gates from scratch and checking each one down to the transistor. Near the end came the fun part, carrying a 5-tap FIR filter from a MATLAB equation to working Verilog.

I derived PMOS drain current equations, explored why an NMOS can’t pass a strong logic high without a threshold drop, built gates from Boolean expressions with De Morgan’s laws, and studied the five regions of the inverter transfer curve. I designed inverters in five sizes (x1 to x16), 2- and 3-input NAND and NOR gates, and a D flip-flop, each with schematic, layout, and LVS check. Measuring delays at 10, 20, and 30 fF loads showed me that larger inverters drive loads faster but cost more area and power.

The flip-flop was the hardest gate by far, since its tightly packed layout took many more tries to pass LVS. For the filter, I built floating-point and fixed-point (Q1.15) MATLAB versions and a Verilog version using registers, Booth multipliers, and an adder tree. The testbench matched the MATLAB reference sample by sample with zero mismatches.

Reflection 4

EE 553: Microelectronics Fabrication Lab

Taught by Professor Jeff Salzmann

Ten lab sessions and a statistical process control (SPC) project gave me hands-on experience with metrology, thin-film deposition, photolithography, and etching. Where other courses focused on circuit design, this one showed me the fabrication processes behind integrated circuits and why process control matters.

We began by measuring airborne particle counts at 25 cleanroom locations, which showed why gowning and sticky mats matter. In metrology we measured the same wafer with a reflectometer, profilometer, and microscope, which taught me to check measurement quality before trusting data. We studied electron-beam evaporation, monitoring chamber pressure, turbo pump speed, and load-lock status in the Kurt J. Lesker eKlipse software, and sputtering, where watching the argon plasma through the viewport connected theory to practice.

Fabrication is sensitive to measurement accuracy, contamination, and process conditions, and the SPC project clarified the difference between a stable process and one that consistently meets specifications.

Work in progress: photolithography and the final project will be added as the course continues.
